# Literature Review

**Task:** DIP-5 · **Status:** draft for pre-defense 1 (24 October 2026)

## 1. Purpose and method

This review answers three questions that shape the system design:

1. **RQ1.** Which computational approaches exist for arranging furniture in a room automatically or semi-automatically?
2. **RQ2.** Which approaches exist for recommending furniture to a user (by style, size, budget, compatibility), including multimodal (image + text) search?
3. **RQ3.** What is known about how consumers use 3D / AR visualisation when choosing furniture, and how such systems should be evaluated?

**Search.** Google Scholar, ACM Digital Library, IEEE Xplore, SpringerLink, ScienceDirect and CVF Open Access were searched with the terms *furniture arrangement*, *indoor scene synthesis*, *layout optimization*, *furniture recommendation*, *visual compatibility*, *constraint-based recommender*, *multimodal product search*, *CLIP retrieval*, *complementary product recommendation*, *approximate nearest neighbour*, *ranking evaluation*, *augmented reality furniture retail*, *usability scale*. Vendor pages and technical documentation were used only for describing tools and standards, never as evidence of scientific claims.

**Inclusion criteria.** (a) Peer-reviewed publication from a recognised publisher or conference (ACM, IEEE, SAGE, PLOS, VLDB Endowment, UXPA, CVF, NeurIPS, ICLR, PMLR), or an official technical standard; preprint servers and unofficial copies are not used; (b) **open access**: the full text is free on the official publisher or proceedings site, so every claim can be checked by the reader; (c) directly relevant to RQ1–RQ3; (d) the core body published 2021–2026 (the field moves fast since the arrival of transformer, diffusion and LLM methods), with foundational works kept where later work builds on them.

**Result.** 26 works are used: 24 scientific publications (numbered [1]–[23], with two papers grouped under [23]; 10 of them from 2021 or later) and 2 technical sources (T1, T2). All 26 are open access on the official publisher, proceedings or standards site (see the links in Section 6). Sources are cited as [n] and listed in Section 6.

## 3. Critical synthesis

### 3.1 Furniture arrangement (RQ1)

Three generations of methods are visible:

1. **Rule- and guideline-based optimisation** [1, 2]. Interior design knowledge (clearances, walkways, facing relations, wall alignment) is written as a cost function and optimised with MCMC or simulated annealing. These methods are *explainable, need no training data and guarantee constraints*, but each rule must be authored and weighted by hand, and results can look generic.
2. **Learned priors from scene datasets** [3, 5, 6, 7]. Models learn where objects usually go from thousands of designed rooms. Quality improved, but they depend on large datasets (SUNCG was withdrawn for licensing reasons, which invalidated much prior work) and they do not guarantee physical validity.
3. **Generative and language-driven models** [12, 15–18]. Transformers, diffusion models and LLMs produce diverse, controllable scenes and accept text instructions. However, they need GPU training on 3D-FRONT [10], the overlap/out-of-bounds problem is reduced but not eliminated [12, 18], and LLM-based pipelines add cost, latency and non-determinism [15, 16].

**What the project takes from this.** A recurring and well-supported pattern is to separate *what should be in the room and how objects relate* from *exact geometric placement*, and to let a deterministic solver guarantee validity [1, 6, 16, 17]. Our design follows it: a rule-based solver (v1, from [1, 2]) places objects and enforces hard constraints (inside room, no overlap, door clearance); the "intelligent" layer proposes items and relations. A learned or LLM model can replace the proposal layer later without changing the validation guarantees.

**Limitation we accept.** Our v1 layout will be less varied than learned models. We judge this acceptable for a diploma prototype because validity and explainability matter more to a buyer than variety, and we will measure validity explicitly (overlap rate, clearance violations) in the evaluation plan.

### 3.2 Furniture recommendation (RQ2)

Collaborative filtering requires interaction history that a new system does not have (cold start); content features and hybrid models are the usual answer [8]. Constraint- and knowledge-based recommenders [9] fit furniture especially well because requirements are explicit and hard (the sofa must physically fit and cost less than the budget) and because they can explain each recommendation. Visual embeddings [4] capture similarity, but similarity is not the same as compatibility: style compatibility between different categories has to be learned or encoded separately [11], and both need labelled data.

**Multimodal search.** Contrastive image–text models such as CLIP [19] map product photos and descriptions into one vector space, so a sofa can be found from a text request ("light sofa up to 2 m") or from the photo of an armchair the user has already chosen. Work on complementary recommendation [20] shows that what matters for furnishing is *compatibility* with items already in the scene, not only visual similarity. Approximate nearest-neighbour indexes (HNSW [21]) make such search fast enough for interactive use on catalogues of thousands of items. Ranking quality is measured with graded-relevance metrics such as nDCG [22].

**What the project takes from this.** The prototype is a constraint-based recommender with weighted soft scoring (style, size ratio to room, price) and a human-readable explanation per item; it also serves as the rule-based reference. The ML service adds CLIP embeddings of images and descriptions (pgvector, HNSW index) as a second signal in a weighted hybrid [8], ranking candidates by room size, budget and compatibility with already chosen furniture. Both are compared with the baseline "filter by category and price" using Precision@K and nDCG@10 [22].

### 3.3 Visualisation and user evaluation (RQ3)

The IKEA Place case [13] shows that the main purchase barrier online is uncertainty about size and fit. A TAM-based study of AR shopping [14] explains the effect of the AR experience on purchase intention through perceived *usefulness* and *ease of use*. This suggests that 3D alone is not enough: the tool must also support the decision (dimensions, cost, alternatives). Our evaluation therefore measures task success and decision confidence, not only satisfaction, and uses SUS [23] for comparability.

## 5. Gaps that justify the project

- Research systems [5–7, 10–18] output layouts but do not connect to a purchasable catalog, prices or the user's budget.
- Commercial tools (see `existing-systems.md`) offer 3D/AR viewing but limited, opaque "AI" with no explanation of why an item fits.
- Few systems combine **explainable recommendations + guaranteed-valid automatic layout + browser-based 3D** in one workflow. This combination is the project's contribution.
